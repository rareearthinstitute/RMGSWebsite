stadium security readiness assessment

RMGS delivered independent threat security assessments supporting a professional sports team's league-mandated, pre-kickoff certification. The non–game-day evaluation identified vulnerabilities across physical security measures and operational procedures at three designated access points — administrative, loading dock, and player entrances. Findings were synthesized into an executive briefing that mapped exposure points, prioritized remediation pathways, and aligned corrective actions with ISO 31000 principles.

emergency preparedness & management

hover →

A closed loop from threat to readiness — characterizing the danger, engineering the countermeasure, then proving the response under pressure.

Preparedness is only real if it has been tested against a thinking adversary. RMGS runs the full arc — from understanding the threat to validating that people, plans, and equipment actually hold up.

Assess

Threat and risk assessment — WMD/CBRNE detection, deterrence, and defeat, plus design-basis-threat analysis that defines what "ready" must mean.

Design

CBRNE-focused product design and the identification, development, and validation of emerging requirements and technologies.

Rehearse

Full-lifecycle exercises against dynamic adversarial and natural-hazard scenarios — testing force and critical-infrastructure protection, response, recovery, and COOP.

Improve

Evaluation and improvement planning that converts exercise findings into corrected procedures and measurable readiness gains.

IT Asset Accountability & End-Use Monitoring — Nuclear Enterprise Mission Assurance

Delivery Order HDTRA123F0101 · DTRA Nuclear Enterprise Directorate · Fort Belvoir, VA

Mission

RMGS serves as DTRA’s internal IT asset accountability and compliance team, functioning as the agency’s End-Use Monitors (EUMs) for all information technology. The team provides the independent oversight that keeps the agency’s IT hardware and software accountable, compliant, and secure across its Nuclear Enterprise mission.

Scope of Responsibilities

The team delivers full-lifecycle IT asset management — inventory management, validation, verification, accountability, and lifecycle oversight of both hardware and software assets supporting Fort Belvoir and satellite locations. As End-Use Monitors, RMGS independently confirms that assets are properly received, documented, tracked, and dispositioned in accordance with agency and federal requirements.

Results Delivered

Within the first six months of performance, the team identified and flagged several million dollars’ worth of inbound equipment containing prohibited components, surfacing significant supply-chain and security concerns before the equipment entered the agency’s environment. The team also uncovered a substantial quantity of hardware that had been misplaced, improperly tracked, incorrectly documented for disposal, or otherwise unaccounted for across the organization. Working closely with the customer, RMGS established a far clearer picture of the agency’s true IT asset inventory and measurably strengthened overall accountability and compliance.

the gap between built and bought

your capital proved it can be built. the customer needs proof it works.

Special-operations buyers don't field capability on a slick demo. They require evidence that a system performs for the operator, in realistic conditions, against a defined standard — generated by someone other than the company selling it. That evidence is the missing link between a great prototype and a program of record.

the flagship problem: too much tech, talking past itself

Dozens of well-funded drones, UAS, counter-UAS systems, sensors, and C2 apps are in the field — and few of them talk to each other. Every new system adds another feed and another screen. More technology is producing more operator load, not less. The requirement is a single pane of glass that fuses every input and lets the operator optimize across tools.

the unanswered question

Whoever claims to deliver that integration faces one unforgiving test: does it actually reduce operator burden and improve mission outcomes — measurably, under realistic conditions, in front of the people who will carry it? RMX exists to answer exactly that, privately, with hard data you own.

bottom line up front

compressed timelines are colliding with expanding threats

The accelerated push to deploy warfighting nuclear energy compresses acquisition, integration, oversight, and security timelines — at the same moment adversaries expand their ability to exploit cyber, physical, institutional, and supply-chain vulnerabilities across critical infrastructure.

certification alone is not enough

Technical certification and compliance validation remain necessary but not sufficient. The question is not only whether systems work as designed, but whether the broader enterprise withstands adaptive adversary pressure, cascading disruption, and institutional friction.

insight has to arrive in time

Red Teams deliver strategic value only when integrated into existing decision cycles. RMGS operates as a mission partner, embedding adversary-informed analysis directly into live acquisition and modernization timelines — before pathways harden.

energy is now a mission system

As energy resilience ties to operational endurance and strategic competition, acquisition decisions must account for how adversaries target the systems, institutions, and infrastructure supporting warfighting energy — not just the reactor.

why RMGS red teams

insight you cannot get from a checklist or a model

What sets RMGS apart is deep operational experience conducting Red Team operations against highly sensitive installations — heritage rooted in the U.S. Navy's Security Coordination Team, the "Original Red Cell," and founding members of Naval Special Warfare Development Group.

inform decisions at the speed of operations

Warfighter-relevant insight, early and continuously, so leaders act within compressed planning and modernization timelines. As a trusted, unbiased partner, RMGS helps decision makers prioritize action rather than merely document risk.

emulate adaptive human adversaries

Our Red Teams think and act like real opponents. They adapt in real time, exploit how defenders respond, and surface vulnerabilities rooted in human behavior, organizational culture, degraded communications, and interagency seams — not isolated technical flaws.

address the human-machine dimension

As adversaries leverage AI and automation, our teams test how humans interpret, trust, or misread machine-generated information under stress — revealing risk from overreliance on automation, compressed timelines, and institutional rigidity.

reveal cascading failure conditions

RMGS Red Teams expose where and why defenses fail and how failures propagate across systems and organizations — insights that emerge only when defenses are stressed by adversary behavior that evolves in response to defensive action.

our approach

threat realism, translated into decisions and dollars

Universal adversary methods and design-based threat analysis turn how a capable opponent could actually behave into acquisition guidance leaders can act on.

How do universal adversary methods support investment decisions?

By modeling exploitation pathways available to any capable, motivated opponent, the universal adversary methodology shifts the focus from who the adversary is to what they can do — avoiding defenses overfit to known actors and revealing vulnerabilities that persist regardless of ideology, geography, or organization. This exposes novel, non-obvious attack pathways, and early insight feeds warfighter-driven investment and policy decisions before acquisition pathways harden.

How does RMGS enable smarter spending and avoid costly redesign?

Design-based threat findings identify which capabilities meaningfully reduce adversary options and which do not, directing resources toward investments that actually strengthen deterrence rather than marginal or symbolic capability. RMGS distinguishes enduring value from hidden risk in legacy systems, justifies procurement against recurring adversary-exploited weaknesses, flags candidates for divestment or redesign, and exposes transitional risk during periods of capability overlap — while building a defensible record of due diligence and informed risk acceptance.

How do insights arrive in time to influence budgets and requirements already in motion?

emerging threat · counter-sUAS

the drone is already inside the threat model

Small unmanned aircraft have become a defining threat to fixed critical infrastructure. Cheap, autonomous, and hard to attribute, they enable standoff surveillance, payload delivery, and coordinated swarming over the exact facilities — reactors, substations, and energy nodes — that warfighting power depends on. A capable adversary will fly the site long before it ever touches the fence, so counter-small-UAS (C-sUAS) belongs in the threat model from day one.

the threat we emulate

Reconnaissance overflights, GPS-denied navigation, sensor and comms interference, payload delivery, and multi-aircraft swarms that saturate defenders — flown the way a capable adversary actually employs them, not a scripted demonstration.

how we red team it

Our operators act as the C-sUAS adversary against detect-track-identify-defeat architectures, then stress the human in the loop: alert fatigue, ambiguous tracks, degraded comms, and the decision latency that turns a detection into a defeat — or a miss.

what the customer learns

Where detection coverage actually breaks, which defeat options survive contact, and whether operators can make the right call fast enough — defensible findings that drive C-sUAS acquisition and siting decisions before the reactor is fielded.

connected capability · RMX

the operator's burden: many drones, one decision

The hardest C-sUAS problem is not any single sensor — it is the operator drowning in feeds from systems that don't talk to each other. RMX, the RMGS private proving ground, runs exactly this case: standing up an instrumented multi-UAS vignette, putting SOF-credible operators on a vendor's integration layer, and producing defensible evidence on whether a true single pane of glass actually reduces operator load and improves outcomes.
